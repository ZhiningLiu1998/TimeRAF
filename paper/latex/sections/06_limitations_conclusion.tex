\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked what a retrieval-augmented time-series forecaster should retrieve, and
answered that it should retrieve its own errors rather than its inputs' futures.
\method{} realizes that answer as a post-hoc revision layer: it keeps the frozen
forecast as the query-specific prior, indexes a chronological memory of that
forecaster's validation residuals by a descriptor built from context and intended
forecast, shrinks corrections whose neighbours disagree, and applies one only
when held-out validation prefers it to a simpler correction or to doing nothing.
With all 585 base forecasts fixed, error retrieval is the most reliable single
object at 490 strict wins, beating the published RAFT and SARAF operators by 115
and 106 settings, and the complete policy reaches 533; the margin widens as the
frozen forecaster becomes more accurate. Applied broadly, \method{} improves
every reported metric in 562/585 settings across 13 architectures and in 81/94
settings across static ensembles, AutoML, and a forecasting foundation model.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
Error memory pays off when a forecaster's mistakes recur in retrievably similar
states. Figure~\ref{fig:object-strength} locates the crossover: when the frozen
forecast is poor, copying an endpoint-aligned analogue is the better object, so we
recommend error retrieval for reasonably accurate forecasters; that post-hoc
dependence is much weaker on EPF. The primary matrix uses one seed, its held-out
split follows execution order inside one benchmark suite, and the prequential
study covers one backbone at one horizon. We do not ablate the descriptor, channel
pooling, or shrinkage, and a retriever that detects event prefixes explicitly is
the next step.
